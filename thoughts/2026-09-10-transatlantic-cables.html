Transatlantic Cables: The Physical Internet Nobody Thinks About

Rowa Taha · September 10, 2026 · 3 min read

Okay so here's a fact that I think about way more than a normal person should, when you email someone across the ocean, or your video call connects to a colleague in London, or you buy something and it needs to talk to a server in another country, that data is almost certainly not going up to a satellite. It's traveling through a physical cable, about the width of a garden hose, lying on the actual floor of the ocean.

The scale of this system is what gets me. There are roughly 600 active and planned submarine cables in the world, spanning about 1.48 million kilometers, connecting to around 1,600 landing stations on shore[1]. And they're not a backup system, they're the main one. Ninety-five percent of international communications and an estimated $10 trillion in daily financial transactions run through these cables[1]. Satellites get all the press when people picture "how the internet works," but the physical, boring, unglamorous cable on the seafloor is actually doing almost all of the work.

Most damage to these cables isn't sabotage, it's mundane. About 200 cable faults happen every year on average, and roughly 70% of that is fishing trawlers or ship anchors dragging across a cable they didn't know was there[1]. Repairs take two to eight weeks and cost half a million to a million dollars each time[1]. That alone should make you a little uneasy about how fragile this is, before you even get to the deliberate version.

And the deliberate version is not hypothetical anymore. Since Russia's invasion of Ukraine, at least 10 subsea cables in the Baltic Sea have been cut or damaged, and earlier this year Russian submarines were detected operating close to critical undersea cables in UK waters[1]. RAND's analysis is careful not to overstate this, sabotaging a cable does take real skill and specialized equipment, it's not something any random actor can casually pull off, but that same analysis is clear that the capability exists among state actors and the incentive to use it is growing[2]. What worries security researchers more than any single cut is the geography, a small number of landing sites carry a hugely disproportionate share of transatlantic traffic, and seventeen cables alone pass through the Red Sea carrying most of the data flow between Europe, Asia, and Africa[1]. That's not resilience, that's a chokepoint with a redundant-sounding name.

Here's the detail that reframed the whole issue for me: the most vulnerable part of a subsea cable isn't the deep ocean stretch, it's the shore. CEPA's analysis argues the real danger is in the littoral zone, where cables make landfall and sit in shallow water near the landing station, because that's where they're easiest to reach and hardest to actually defend[3]. We tend to picture cable sabotage as some dramatic deep-sea operation, when the actual soft spot is a stretch of shallow water near a beach that most people walk past without a second thought.

When Taiwan had two cables cut in 2023, banking and communications were seriously disrupted. When Nigeria had a multi-day outage from a cable fault in 2024, the estimated loss was around $600 million[1]. These aren't abstract infrastructure numbers, they're what happens to ordinary people and businesses when a piece of physical hardware nobody thinks about gets damaged, on purpose or by accident, and there's no redundant path ready to pick up the slack.
