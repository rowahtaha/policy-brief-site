Australia Just Told Big Tech: Pay Eight Publishers, Not Just One

Rowa Taha · September 28, 2026 · 3 min read

Australia already had a news bargaining law. This is the version lawmakers wrote after watching platforms find a way around the first one. Under the original 2021 code, Google and Meta could cut a handful of convenient deals with a few big outlets, point to them as proof of good-faith compliance, and never actually face formal designation or consequences. The new News Media Bargaining Charge Bill, which cleared Parliament on Aug. 20 and took effect a week later, closes that loophole with a number instead of a negotiation: any platform earning more than A$250 million in Australian digital ad revenue now has to strike deals with at least eight separate news companies, or pay a flat 2.75% levy on that revenue[1][2].

What I find genuinely interesting isn't the levy, it's the "at least eight" requirement. One deal with one major outlet doesn't cut it anymore. That's a deliberate design choice meant to push bargaining leverage down to mid-size and regional Australian newsrooms, the ones that never had the negotiating power to get a platform's attention on their own. It's a small structural fix with a real effect: it turns "did you pay journalism" from a headline-friendly yes or no into a question of how many newsrooms actually benefited.

The rate itself also moved up during the legislative process, 2.75% instead of the 2.5% originally proposed[1][3]. Levies aimed at powerful platforms usually get negotiated down before they pass, not up. That tells me something about how much political appetite currently exists in Australia for holding the line, not just gesturing at it.

I'm curious to see which platforms end up paying the levy outright versus doing the work of signing eight real deals, because that choice will say more about which companies actually value Australian journalism than the law itself ever could.
