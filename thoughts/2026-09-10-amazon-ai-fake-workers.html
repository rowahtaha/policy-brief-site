How AI Is Changing the Way People Deal With Each Other at Work

Rowa Taha · September 10, 2026 · 3 min read

I was at a talk recently where someone described using AI at her company to catch people trying to scam their way into a job, and it stuck with me because it's not really a story about fraud, it's a story about what it now takes to trust that the person on your video call is who they say they are.

Amazon's Chief Security Officer put actual numbers on this. Since April 2024, Amazon has blocked more than 1,800 suspected applicants tied to North Korea, and the pace is picking up, 27% more of these applications quarter over quarter this year[1]. The company uses what they describe as "AI-powered screening with human verification," checking applicants against nearly 200 high-risk institutions, flagging application anomalies and geographic inconsistencies, on top of the usual background checks and interviews[1].

Here's the part that actually changes how I think about this. It's not that a few people are lying on their résumés. It's a systematized operation. North Korean operatives apply for remote tech jobs using fabricated or stolen identities, use AI tools to polish résumés and LinkedIn profiles, and increasingly use real-time deepfake video during the actual interview to impersonate someone else's face while they talk[1]. In August, eleven allied governments, the US, Japan, South Korea, and for the first time France, Germany, Italy, and the Netherlands, issued a joint warning that this specific tactic, live AI deepfakes in job interviews, is now widespread enough to warrant a coordinated international alert[2]. There's a TechCrunch clip going around of an actual interviewer catching one of these in real time, watching the video glitch in exactly the way a face-swap filter glitches when someone turns their head too fast[3]. Once you've seen what to look for, unnatural hairline boundaries, a half-second of lip and audio mismatch, you can't unsee it, but most hiring managers have never been told to look.

The scale is the part that surprised me most. This isn't a handful of scammers. At least 136 companies have been confirmed affected, Okta has identified more than 6,500 similar cases globally, and the US Treasury estimated the scheme generated nearly $800 million in 2024 alone, money that reportedly helps fund North Korea's weapons programs[4]. These aren't people trying to get a paycheck under false pretenses in the ordinary sense, once hired, a large share of the salary gets routed back to the state.

So think about what this does to an ordinary hiring manager, someone doing a normal remote interview for a normal engineering role. They now have to wonder, at least a little, whether the face on their screen is really attached to the voice, whether the résumé was written by the person or an AI trying to make a fake identity sound plausible, whether the "US-based" candidate is actually sitting at a laptop farm run by an American who's renting out their address to make it look that way[1]. That's a genuinely strange thing to have to hold in your head during a job interview. AI didn't just make it easier to fake being a real employee. It made it necessary to build AI just to check whether the person applying is real, which means the trust that used to be the baseline of a hiring conversation now has to be actively verified, every time, by a machine watching for the tells a human eye would miss.
