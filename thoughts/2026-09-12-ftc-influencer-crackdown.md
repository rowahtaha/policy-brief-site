The FTC has made influencer disclosure its top social-media enforcement priority for 2026[1], and its own Endorsement Guides spell out the standard plainly: if there's a connection between an endorser and a brand that a significant minority of consumers wouldn't expect, it has to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously[4]. In December, the FTC backed that up with a real enforcement move, warning letters to 10 companies over possible violations of its Consumer Review Rule, an early signal it's shifting from guidance to active enforcement[5]. That's the regulatory side. But the bigger financial pressure right now isn't actually coming from the government, it's coming from private plaintiffs filing class actions directly, and they're not small. Celsius Holdings is facing a suit alleging damages of at least $450 million, filed against the company and three influencers who promoted its products without adequately disclosing the paid relationship. Shein is facing a suit from residents across three states over roughly the same pattern, seven influencers accused of either skipping disclosure entirely or burying it somewhere nobody would actually read it, with damages sought over $500 million. Revolve is facing its own $50 million suit over paying creators to promote products without clear disclosure[2][3]. Add in the suits against Alo Yoga and Beach Bunny, and the combined damages being sought across just five companies top $1.1 billion[3].

Here's what actually counts as a violation, because "burying" disclosure is doing a lot of work in these complaints and I think it's worth being specific about what that means. A tiny "#ad" hidden at the bottom of a caption, below a wall of hashtags nobody scrolls to. A disclosure only inside a video nobody's watching to the end. Relying entirely on a platform's built-in tag, like Instagram's "Paid Partnership" label, instead of saying it plainly in the creator's own words within the actual content. The FTC has been explicit that the platform-native tools don't satisfy the requirement on their own, they can supplement a real disclosure, they can't replace one[1].

What's new in the 2026 wave specifically is who's on the hook. Enforcement used to focus almost entirely on brands. Now individual creators are being named directly, at scale, not just the companies paying them[1]. And the definition of what counts as a paid endorsement keeps widening too, it's not just a sponsored post anymore, reposts, tags, and even likes can count as an endorsement requiring disclosure under updated guidance[1].

I think the throughline here connects to something bigger than influencer marketing specifically. It's the same basic question as data breach notification or AI content labeling: do you actually have a right to know when you're being sold something, versus told something honestly? An influencer's genuine opinion and a paid placement dressed up as one look identical from the outside. The only thing that tells them apart is disclosure, done clearly, not buried.

This is also, structurally, a state-by-state question in practice, since a lot of the private class actions are being brought under individual state consumer-protection statutes, not just federal FTC authority.
