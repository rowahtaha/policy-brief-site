Report on this one felt right, because the actual mechanics of India's new rule are more aggressive than most of what I've covered on AI content labeling, and I don't think enough people know it exists yet.

On February 10, 2026, India's Ministry of Electronics and IT notified an amendment to the IT Rules, 2021, creating a formal legal category called "synthetically generated information," or SGI, covering any audio, image, video, or combination of them that was created or altered algorithmically to look or sound like a real person or a real event[1]. Effective February 20, 2026, all of that content has to carry a visible disclaimer plus permanent provenance metadata baked in, and platforms have to run automated detection tools to catch it[1].

Here's the part that actually stopped me. If a platform gets a valid takedown request for illegal synthetic content and doesn't act within three hours, it immediately loses its Safe Harbor protection under Indian law[1]. Three hours. Not three days, not "reasonable time," a number small enough that it basically forces platforms to build automated response systems instead of relying on human moderators reviewing a queue. Freshfields' analysis of the amendment calls out how far this goes beyond what most jurisdictions have attempted, this isn't a labeling suggestion, it's a structural compliance obligation with a deadline that can end a platform's legal protection if missed[2].

Every intermediary operating in India, social platforms, video sites, messaging apps, hosting providers, and AI tools themselves, has to revisit its entire compliance posture against this[3]. That's a genuinely huge lift for anyone operating at scale in a country with over 800 million internet users.

I keep coming back to the tradeoff buried in this. A three-hour window is fast enough to actually matter, a deepfake that goes viral usually does most of its damage in the first few hours, so speed is the right instinct. But it also means platforms have every incentive to over-remove anything that looks remotely synthetic rather than risk their Safe Harbor status, which is its own kind of chilling effect, just aimed at content moderation instead of at government critics directly. I don't think there's a clean answer here. I think it's worth watching how Indian platforms actually implement this over the next few months, whether the automated detection tools are accurate enough not to sweep up real content by mistake.

Either way, this is now one of the most concrete AI-labeling laws on the books anywhere, and I think it's worth knowing whether your own country has anything close to it.
