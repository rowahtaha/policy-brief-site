The UK (and Australia) Are Rewriting the Rules for Kids Online

Rowa Taha · September 12, 2026 · 2 min read

The UK and Australia have become some of the only countries in the world that actually offer minors special legal protection online, not just guidance, not just a platform's own terms of service, actual law. I think that's worth sitting with, because it means most countries still don't.

Here's what the UK just did. On June 15, 2026, the government announced a full ban on social media use for anyone under 16[1]. It's more aggressive than Australia's earlier version too, blocking specific functions like livestreaming and messaging with strangers, not just account creation[2]. A month later, on July 15, the government added a second layer: default overnight curfews, midnight to 6am, automatically switched on for 16 and 17 year olds across social apps, along with turning off the features specifically designed to keep people scrolling longer[2]. The legal mechanism running underneath all of this is the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, which slots a new section directly into the existing Online Safety Act 2023[2].

None of it is law yet, to be precise about where things actually stand. The regulations still need approval from both Houses of Parliament, and the government's own target is to have them in force by spring 2027[1][2]. In the meantime, Ofcom has been asked to run a rapid study on what actually counts as effective age verification for confirming someone is over 16, due by the end of October 2026[2], which is its own genuinely hard problem, since most age-verification methods either don't work well or require handing over more personal data than feels proportionate to the risk you're trying to prevent.

I don't think this is a simple good-news story, and I don't want to write it like one. Age verification systems create their own privacy exposure. A ban this broad will get worked around by plenty of teenagers the same way every previous restriction has. But I also think it's genuinely significant that a government looked at the current state of kids' social media use and decided the answer wasn't another advisory panel, it was actual binding law with actual mechanisms behind it, curfews that are on by default rather than opt-in, functions disabled rather than merely discouraged.

That's rare. Most countries haven't done anything close to this. I built the question directly into the Digital Rights Atlas, whether minors need verified parental consent or age assurance for social media, cited, country by country, and for most of the 195 countries I've covered, there's genuinely nothing on the books.
