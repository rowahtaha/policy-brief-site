A $21 Million Lawsuit Over One Opinion Piece

Rowa Taha · September 12, 2026 · 2 min read

Journalists get harassed all over the world for doing their jobs, and I don't think most people realize how often the harassment now comes dressed up as a normal lawsuit.

Here's what actually happened. On July 3, 2026, veteran Malaysian journalist P. Gunasegaram published a piece on his own site, KINIBIZ, questioning the costs and delays on the LRT3 Shah Alam rail line, and shared it on Facebook[1]. That's it. That's the article. Malaysian Resources Corporation Berhad, the company behind the project, and its executive vice-chairman Salim Fateh Din, filed suit in the Shah Alam High Court on July 29, seeking RM100 million, about US$24.45 million, in damages, plus an injunction ordering Gunasegaram to take the article down and barring him from publishing anything similar[1]. They're arguing the piece falsely implied the project was awarded through cronyism and that it hurt the company's share price.

He's defended it as fair comment and refused to retract or apologize[1]. And this is where the story becomes bigger than one company and one journalist. The Committee to Protect Journalists publicly called on MRCB and Salim Fateh Din to withdraw the suit, describing the RM100 million figure as "excessive" and naming it directly as an example of the kind of legal action meant to punish, not to genuinely seek accountability for reputational harm[2]. That's the textbook definition of a SLAPP, a Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation, a suit filed less to win in court and more to make the cost of speaking up so high that nobody does it again.

What strikes me about this specific case is the scale mismatch. A major listed company with a vice-chairman backing it, going after one columnist over one piece of financial commentary, seeking damages large enough to end most journalists' careers regardless of whether the underlying claims about cronyism were even provable. Malaysiakini's coverage of the case lays out just how directly this ties to a specific infrastructure project people are entitled to ask questions about, public transit spending is about as legitimate a subject for scrutiny as journalism gets[3].

Most countries don't have a real answer to this. An anti-SLAPP law, a legal mechanism that lets a journalist get an obviously retaliatory suit thrown out fast, before it bankrupts them in legal fees alone, is still rare. I built that exact question into the Digital Rights Atlas, whether journalists in a given country are actually protected from being sued into silence, and for most of the 195 countries I've covered, the honest answer is no protection exists at all.
