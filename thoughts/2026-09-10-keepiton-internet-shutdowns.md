This one ties directly into a lot of what I've been writing about lately, because internet access keeps showing up as the thing governments reach for the moment they want to control what people can see, say, or organize around.

Access Now started the #KeepItOn campaign in 2016, and this year marks its tenth anniversary[1]. The coalition itself has grown into something genuinely large, more than 366 national, international, regional, and local organizations working together, documenting shutdowns through a shared tracking project, running a 24/7 Digital Security Helpline for journalists and human rights defenders caught in the middle of one, and pushing governments and companies toward accountability[1]. Ten years in, and the thing they're fighting hasn't slowed down, it's gotten worse.

The 2025 numbers are genuinely hard to read past without stopping. At least 313 shutdowns across 52 countries, which is actually an increase over 2024's already-record 304 shutdowns in 55 countries[2]. The #KeepItOn Global Campaign Manager, Felicia Anthonio, put it plainly: "Year after year, authorities seek the power to influence elections, silence and isolate people, and attack our rights with impunity"[2]. Myanmar led the world for the second year running, with 95 documented shutdowns in a single year, essentially a shutdown every four days[2]. And seven countries showed up on the list for the first time in 2025: Albania, Angola, Cambodia, Lithuania, Panama, Papua New Guinea, and, notably, the United States[2]. That last one is the detail that made me stop scrolling.

Conflict was the single biggest trigger, responsible for 125 shutdowns across 14 countries, about 40% of the global total, and at least 70 shutdowns coincided with documented grave human rights abuses, murder, torture, rape, war crimes, across 21 countries[2]. That correlation is the part that matters most to me. A shutdown isn't just an inconvenience, it's frequently the thing that happens right before or during the worst violence, specifically because cutting the connection makes it harder for anyone outside to see what's happening and harder for anyone inside to call for help.

There's a newer trend in the report that I hadn't clocked before researching this: shutdowns targeting satellite internet specifically. Fourteen LEO satellite shutdowns across seven countries in 2025, up from just four the year before[2]. As more people route around ground-based shutdowns using satellite connections, the people ordering the shutdowns are starting to go after that workaround too, which tells you this isn't a static fight, it's an arms race between people trying to stay connected and governments trying to stop them.

Not everything in the report is bad news. Bangladesh took steps toward actually outlawing shutdowns, and South Sudan rescinded its social media ban[2]. Small wins, but real ones, and they're the kind of thing that only happens because an organization spent ten years building the documentation and the pressure to make a government walk something back. I don't think #KeepItOn gets to declare victory anytime soon given where the numbers are headed, but I do think this is the clearest example I've come across of what sustained, unglamorous advocacy work actually looks like, tracking every single shutdown, one country at a time, for a decade, so that "the internet just happened to go down" stops being a plausible cover story for anyone.
