Issue No. 8 · Week of October 12–18, 2026

Ten Stories You Haven't Read Here Before

By Rowa Taha

No data centers, no Meta, no OpenAI, no Google-vs-Brussels. This week is the rest of the world: a court overruling a shutdown, a province placed under emergency rule, a hijab ban that turned into a platform block, and a legislature making chip smuggling a crime instead of a fine.

01
Internet Freedom · India

Delhi Shut Off the Internet to Stop a Protest. The Supreme Court Said No.

India's Ministry of Home Affairs ordered telecom operators (Reliance Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea and state-run MTNL) to suspend mobile internet service in a 4-kilometer radius around central Delhi for 24 hours starting 10 p.m. on Oct. 9, timed precisely to a planned protest at Jantar Mantar demanding the resignation of the Chief Election Commissioner over allegations of electoral-roll manipulation.[1] A second, narrower order cut connectivity around eight detention centers the same evening.[1] This is the version of "national security" justification that should make everyone uneasy. Not a terrorism threat, not a riot already underway. A government decided in advance that the right response to a protest about election integrity was to make sure fewer people could document, coordinate, or even learn about it in real time.

It didn't survive contact with the judiciary. On Oct. 11, a three-judge Supreme Court bench (Chief Justice Surya Kant alongside Justices Joymalya Bagchi and Vipul M. Pancholi) ordered that internet service in Delhi function unhindered, effectively nullifying the shutdown order days before its stated justification could even be tested.[2] That wasn't the end of it. As of Oct. 15, India's Home Secretary was still defending the shutdown's "proportionality" to the Court, insisting the measure was a calibrated response rather than the blunt instrument it looked like from the outside.[2] A government doesn't keep defending a decision after a court has already reversed it unless it's hoping to use the same tool again.

The sequencing is the real story here. The protest was about alleged manipulation of the electoral rolls, a question of whether the mechanics of democracy can be trusted. The government's answer to a protest about electoral integrity was to cut the communications infrastructure that lets citizens verify what's happening to their own democracy in real time. The Supreme Court stepping in isn't a reason to relax. It's a reminder that in India, as in most of the countries this newsletter covers, the default lean of the state is toward control first and justification later, and it takes an active judiciary, not a passive one, to make "proportionality" mean something more than a word in a legal brief.[3]

02
Trade / Chips · Taiwan

Taiwan Moves to Make Smuggling AI Chips to China a Crime, Not Just a Fine

Right now, if someone in Taiwan smuggles advanced AI chips or servers to China, the government's options are limited to warnings or prosecution under laws that were never written with semiconductors in mind. Smuggling itself isn't currently a distinct crime under the island's export-control regime.[1] Economy Minister Kung Ming-hsin announced Oct. 16 that his ministry will move to close that gap, amending the Foreign Trade Act so unauthorized exports of advanced AI chips and the servers built around them become criminally prosecutable, with prison terms of up to five years, replacing a civil-penalty structure currently capped around NT$3 million, pocket change against the value of the hardware being moved.[2]

This isn't Taiwan acting alone. The change fulfills a specific, binding commitment under the Taiwan-US Agreement on Reciprocal Trade signed Feb. 12, 2026, which traded American tariff relief for a tighter Taiwanese export-control regime, the kind of quiet bilateral trade-off that ends up mattering more than either headline on its own.[1] Separately, DPP legislator Chung Chia-pin is pushing an even narrower "mainland China semiconductor chip clause" specifically targeting the loophole exploited by shell-company transshipment routes.[3] Whether this actually passes is still an open question: the Legislative Yuan hasn't yet ratified the underlying Taiwan-US trade agreement this provision is meant to implement, so the criminal penalties could be waiting on a separate legislative fight before they ever take effect.

This matters less for the chips themselves than for what it says about where enforcement pressure on AI hardware is actually landing. The U.S. has spent two years building an export-control wall around China's access to advanced compute. Taiwan criminalizing smuggling reinforces that wall from the inside: the country that actually manufactures the chips has decided that civil fines were never going to deter buyers willing to pay a premium for what they can't legally get. A warning letter doesn't compete with the margins on a smuggled AI server. A five-year prison sentence might.

03
Governance · Pakistan

Pakistan's President Imposes Emergency Rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

President Asif Ali Zardari imposed emergency rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa on Sunday, Oct. 11, invoking Articles 232 and 234 of Pakistan's Constitution on the advice of Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif. It's the mechanism that lets the federal government effectively take over a province's administration when it decides the provincial government can no longer govern.[1] The official justification: the KP government, run by Imran Khan's PTI, had shown what federal officials called "deliberate disengagement" from counterterrorism cooperation amid rising militant activity in the province.[1] Whatever the merits of that security concern, "the opposition-run province wasn't cooperating with us" is also precisely the kind of justification that lets a federal government dissolve a rival party's hold on power without ever having to win an argument about policy.

PTI chairman Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari, notably the son of the president who just signed the order and now leading the party on the other side of this fight, responded by making the emergency's end conditional: he said the measure would be lifted only once the opposition agrees to engage on a joint counterterrorism policy, turning what was framed as a security intervention into an explicit political bargaining chip.[2] That's the tell. If emergency rule were genuinely about an urgent security gap, lifting it would depend on the security situation improving, not on a rival political party agreeing to a specific policy framework.

This isn't really a story about counterterrorism policy. It's a story about how federal emergency powers, nominally neutral tools meant for genuine crises, keep getting reached for as the fastest way to sideline an opposition-run provincial government, in Pakistan and plenty of other places this newsletter has covered. The actual test isn't whether KP's security situation was real. It's whether the emergency gets lifted the moment it stops being politically useful, regardless of what happens with the underlying militancy.

04
Internet Freedom / Human Rights · Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan Blocked TikTok, Instagram and X Over a Hijab Ban

Starting Oct. 12, new Azerbaijani school uniform regulations barred hijab-wearing students and teachers from classrooms nationwide, and the backlash was immediate. Protests broke out across the country, and Azerbaijan's Interior Ministry reported 37 people detained in Baku alone on the first day.[1] Within days, NetBlocks confirmed that TikTok, Instagram and X were blocked across multiple Azerbaijani internet providers, the government's go-to response once a protest starts generating real momentum online.[1]

This is the fourth time Azerbaijan has restricted major platforms in six years, but researchers flag it as meaningfully different from the prior three. Earlier blocks were tied to security operations or political crackdowns the government could frame as emergencies; this is reportedly the first time a platform restriction has been triggered directly by domestic protests over a social policy, rather than a security pretext.[2] That distinction matters. It's a more honest signal of what these blackouts are actually for: not stopping a threat, but stopping people from organizing against a policy the government doesn't want to reverse.

The hijab ban itself is opening an unexpected diplomatic rift, too. Azerbaijan's close ally Turkey, where headscarf politics carry their own fraught history, has reportedly pushed back on the policy, putting Baku in the odd position of defending a restriction that's straining one of its most important regional relationships.[3] The platform block is the more revealing story than the ban itself. A government confident its policy was popular wouldn't need to cut off TikTok to manage the reaction to it.

05
Legislation · Philippines

The Philippines' Cybersecurity Bill Clears the House, 198-3-1

House Bill 9605, the National Cybersecurity and Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Act of 2026, passed the Philippine House of Representatives on Aug. 12 by a lopsided 198-3-1 vote and is now before the Senate.[1] The bill consolidates 28 separate cybersecurity proposals that had been floating around Congress into one framework, and it's a LEDAC priority measure under President Marcos Jr., meaning the executive branch has formally flagged it as something it wants moving fast.[2]

What it actually does: creates a dedicated National Cybersecurity Agency, and requires operators of critical information infrastructure (energy, water, finance, telecom) to run regular risk assessments and continuous monitoring, file audit reports at least once every two years, and promptly report critical or high-risk incidents rather than sitting on them.[3] That reporting requirement is the part that actually has teeth day to day. A risk assessment nobody has to act on is a compliance exercise, but a mandatory incident-reporting clock is the difference between a breach becoming public in days versus years, the way too many breaches currently do.

A 198-3-1 House vote is about as close to consensus as legislatures get, which tells you this isn't a controversial bill in the way AI governance or platform-liability fights usually are. Infrastructure security is one of the rare areas where "the grid and the banks need to not get hacked" clears partisan lines everywhere. The real test is the Senate, and then whether a brand-new National Cybersecurity Agency actually gets the staffing and authority to enforce the incident-reporting requirement, rather than becoming one more agency with a mandate and no teeth.

Accountability · South Africa

Eight Years for an Inside Job That Tried to Extort a Payment Company

A Bellville Specialised Commercial Crimes Court sentenced 36-year-old Lucky Majangandile Erasmus to eight years in prison this month (three suspended, meaning five years actually served) after convicting him on 17 charges connected to an extortion attempt against payment-services provider Ecentric Payment Systems.[1] Erasmus, working with a company insider, installed unauthorized software that gave them remote access to Ecentric's systems, letting them steal sensitive data and change senior managers' passwords.[2]

The ransom demand itself, made back on Nov. 14, 2023, was blunt: pay $534,260 within 16 hours, or the stolen data, including material that could be leaked to competitors and regulators, would become public within 30 hours.[2] That three-year gap between the extortion attempt and the sentence is its own data point. Erasmus's conviction is one of the first publicly reported prosecutions under South Africa's 2021 Cybercrimes Act, and getting a case like this from attempted extortion to an actual prison sentence took the better part of three years, a long time for a company to live with the knowledge that its most sensitive data could surface at any point while the case worked through the courts.[3]

South Africa's privacy framework runs on two tracks: POPIA governs how companies have to handle your data (including a standing 30-day deadline to respond to data erasure or correction requests), and the newer Cybercrimes Act is what actually puts people in prison when someone weaponizes a company's own systems against it. That combination, a regulatory floor plus real criminal exposure, is closer to the accountability model this newsletter keeps asking for elsewhere: not just a fine a company can budget for, but personal criminal liability for the person who actually did it.

07
Trade · China

China Proposes Export Controls on 23 AI Technologies

China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology jointly issued Announcement No. 48 on Oct. 16, proposing to add 40 technologies to the country's restricted and prohibited export catalogue, 23 of them AI-related, covering large language models, AI agents, the compute-power clusters that train them, and related information-processing systems, alongside 12 photovoltaic and 5 biomedicine technologies.[1] Public comment runs through Nov. 15, but this isn't purely a future-tense proposal: an interim registration requirement for covered technologies took effect the same day the announcement was published, meaning companies are already operating under new disclosure obligations while the broader rule is still being finalized.[2]

There's a notable carve-out: technology already made public through open-source release or academic publication is explicitly exempted from the restricted list.[2] That's a meaningful design choice. It means China isn't trying to claw back control over research that's already circulating freely, only over the proprietary, not-yet-public AI capabilities that would otherwise be a one-way transfer of frontier capability out of the country.

The timing lines up with, and mirrors, the export-control architecture the U.S. has spent two years building around China's access to advanced chips, except running in the opposite direction: Washington restricts what reaches China, and this is Beijing restricting what leaves it.[3] Put next to this issue's Taiwan story, you get a fuller picture of the same fight from three different governments: the U.S. controlling chip exports to China, Taiwan criminalizing the smuggling of chips to China, and China now controlling the export of the AI technology built on top of whatever compute it already has. Nobody in this triangle is leaving frontier AI capability to the open market.

Data Rights · India

India's Supreme Court Won't Say Yet Whether the Government Can Exempt Itself From Its Own Privacy Law

A group of petitioners, including Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative access-to-information advocate Venkatesh Nayak, journalist Nitin Sethi, the Reporters' Collective Trust, the National Campaign for People's Right to Information, and transparency activists Geeta Seshu and Anjali Bhardwaj, are challenging a specific provision of India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act: Section 17(2), which lets the central government exempt its own agencies from the law's requirements entirely.[1] A related amendment narrowing the Right to Information Act is bundled into the same challenge.

The same three-judge bench handling the Delhi internet-shutdown case (Chief Justice Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi, and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi) heard this one too, and did the same thing it did there: declined to rule directly, instead referring the matter to a larger bench.[1] The petitioners' core argument is that Section 17(2) doesn't just create a narrow carve-out, it builds a structural loophole that lets government agencies process citizens' data with none of the oversight private companies face, what one filing characterizes as the legal architecture for a surveillance regime with no built-in check.[2] No specific date has yet been confirmed for when the larger bench will actually take up the case.

The pattern across both of this week's India stories is the same: a government reaching for a tool, whether a shutdown order or a self-exemption clause, that concentrates power in its own hands, and a judiciary that's willing to push back on the shutdown in real time but is moving more cautiously on the structural question of whether the government can simply exempt itself from the privacy law it wrote. A law that protects everyone except the government that enforces it isn't really a privacy law in the way most people would understand the term. It's a liability shield with your name on the cover page.

AI Governance · South Korea

South Korea Built a Narrow Legal Door for Training AI on Your Data, Not a Wide One

South Korea's amended Personal Information Protection Act passed the National Assembly on Aug. 20 and took effect Sept. 11, except for the specific provision allowing personal data to be repurposed for AI development, which is deliberately delayed to March 9, 2027, giving regulators and companies more than a year to prepare before it actually activates.[1] The mechanism is precise by design, and not the blanket permission it could easily be mistaken for: it isn't an opt-out system, and companies can't simply decide on their own that their use case qualifies.

Instead, it requires case-by-case pre-approval from South Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission, and only when all three conditions are met: anonymization or pseudonymization genuinely isn't sufficient for the specific AI development purpose, decree-standard technical and administrative safeguards are already in place, and the purpose serves the public interest or data-subject protection with a markedly low risk of harm.[2] That's a meaningfully higher bar than "we need the data and we promise to be careful." Every one of those three conditions has to hold before a company even gets to ask.

A separate, related amendment to South Korea's AI Basic Act, requiring conversational AI systems to disclose that users aren't talking to a human, is moving through the National Assembly alongside this provision, landing in roughly the same window.[3] Taken together, South Korea is trying to thread a genuinely difficult needle: unlocking enough data access that its domestic AI industry can compete with the U.S. and China, without just handing companies a blank check to repurpose whatever personal data they're already sitting on. Whether a pre-approval requirement with three strict conditions actually slows anything down in practice, or becomes a rubber stamp once the Commission has approved the first few applications, is worth watching once March 2027 actually arrives.

AI Governance · Kenya

A Smaller, More Specific African Regulator Forum Than It First Looked

A five-day forum in Nairobi, Oct. 5–9, brought together ICT regulators for the eighth cohort of the ICT Policy and Regulation – Institutional Strengthening Programme (iPRIS), hosted by Kenya's Communications Authority.[1] The attending list is narrower than "African ICT regulators meet" suggests at first glance: regulators from Sierra Leone, Mauritius, Namibia, Zimbabwe, Tanzania and Liberia, plus three regional regulatory bodies: CRASA for Southern Africa, EACO for East Africa, and WATRA for West Africa.[2]

The agenda covered AI governance, digital-platform regulation, next-generation network standards, cybersecurity, competition policy, online safety, data governance and digital inclusion, a genuinely comprehensive list, but a capacity-building list, not a lawmaking one.[1] Nothing coming out of iPRIS cohort eight is binding on any of the attending countries; it's training and institutional coordination, the unglamorous groundwork that has to happen before any of these regulators can actually write or enforce the AI-specific rules this newsletter spends most weeks covering.

This one is worth including deliberately even though it's not a ruling or a passed law, because the gap that matters issue after issue is the one between a country having digital policy on paper and having a regulator that can actually run it. Six countries and three regional bodies spending a week on exactly that capacity is less dramatic than a fine or a court order, but it's the actual infrastructure that makes future enforcement possible, or doesn't, if programs like this stay a once-a-year training exercise instead of building standing institutional capacity.[3]
