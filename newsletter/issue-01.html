Issue No. 1 · Week of August 25–29, 2026

Congress Stalls. Courts and States Are Regulating Big Tech Instead.

By Rowa Taha

WASHINGTON — This week made one thing clear: while Congress debates, courts and state legislatures are doing the actual work of regulating Big Tech. Here's what happened, and what it means.

1. Meta agrees to $16.68 billion settlement over teen safety claims

Meta disclosed in an August 26 court filing that it will pay up to $16.68 billion to resolve claims from a coalition of 29 states accusing the company of designing Facebook and Instagram to be addictive to minors, misrepresenting platform safety, and unlawfully collecting children's data. As part of the deal, Meta must roll out daily time caps and overnight restrictions for teen accounts, strengthen age verification, and expand parental controls — nationwide, not just in the settling states. Meta denies wrongdoing. Investors didn't seem worried: the stock rose 4.4% in premarket trading the next morning.

2. AI transparency law goes live — in California and Brussels, not D.C.

Two major AI disclosure regimes became operative on the same day, August 2: the next phase of the EU AI Act's transparency requirements, and California's AI Transparency Act (SB 942, as amended by AB 853). California's law — deliberately timed to align with the EU's — now requires generative AI providers with more than one million monthly users to offer a free AI-content detection tool and embed both visible and hidden ("latent") disclosures in AI-generated images, video, and audio. Violations run $5,000 per day, per violation. There is no federal equivalent on the table with anywhere near this specificity.

3. Big Tech antitrust: a mixed scoreboard

The FTC's appeal of its lost monopolization case against Meta — a district court ruled last November that Meta isn't a monopolist because it competes with TikTok and YouTube — is still working its way through the courts. Meanwhile, separate antitrust actions against Amazon and Apple are heading toward an October 2026 trial date. Four years into the most aggressive antitrust push against tech platforms in a generation, the government's win rate on the hardest theories — the forward-looking, structural ones — remains shaky.

4. Congress talks, agencies move

The "Great American AI Act of 2026" — which would impose transparency reporting, incident disclosure, and whistleblower protections on frontier AI developers — remains stuck in debate. But FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson has signaled the agency is gearing up for a sharp increase in privacy enforcement in the second half of 2026, especially if the SECURE Data Act moves. His quote: "I think in the second half of 2026, you're going to have a hard time keeping up with the number of cases we're gonna be bringing."

Quick hit: A federal judge ruled the Pentagon cannot blacklist Anthropic from defense contracting over the company's AI safety red lines — an early test case for how far the government can go in punishing AI labs for guardrails it doesn't like.

What This Means

Look at this week's stories side by side and a pattern jumps out: none of the real action happened in Congress.

The Meta settlement is a good example. $16.68 billion dwarfs almost any penalty the FTC has ever extracted from a tech company through formal rulemaking or litigation — and it came from state attorneys general, not a federal privacy statute. The U.S. still has no comprehensive federal privacy law. In its absence, state AG coalitions and private litigation have become the de facto enforcement mechanism for "how tech companies are allowed to treat kids' data" — a patchwork built case by case, settlement by settlement, rather than through a single statute that sets the rule up front.

The same dynamic is playing out in AI governance, just with a different set of actors. Nobody is waiting for Washington. California and the EU moved in lockstep on AI content-labeling requirements, and the timing wasn't an accident — California's amendment was written specifically to line up with the EU AI Act's enforcement clock. That's the "Brussels effect" (and now, arguably, a "Sacramento effect") in action: whoever regulates first, and regulates a large enough market, ends up setting the de facto national — or global — standard, because it's cheaper for a company to build one compliant product than fifty non-compliant ones. If you're advising a company on AI deployment right now, the operative rulebook isn't a federal AI law that doesn't exist — it's SB 942 plus the EU AI Act, full stop.

Antitrust tells a more cautionary story about the limits of this approach. Unlike a privacy settlement, you can't out-litigate your way to breaking up a company at the state level — federal antitrust law requires federal courts, and federal courts have been skeptical of the government's newer, more forward-looking monopoly theories. Meta's trial win, now on appeal, suggests that even a well-resourced, politically bipartisan effort to rein in platform power runs into a structural wall when Congress hasn't updated antitrust law for the platform era. The Amazon and Apple trials this fall will be the next test of whether that wall holds.

Put together, the throughline for the rest of 2026 is this: expect state legislatures, state AGs, and federal agencies to keep filling the space that a gridlocked Congress has left open — through settlements, transparency mandates, and enforcement sweeps rather than new statutes. That's good news if you want faster action. It's a headache if you're a company (or a lawyer) trying to comply with fifty different rulebooks instead of one. And it's worth watching closely if you care about who ends up writing the rules for AI and platform accountability by default, simply because they moved first.

Worth watching next: the Amazon/Apple antitrust trials (October 2026), whether the Great American AI Act gets a floor vote, movement on the SECURE Data Act, and the first enforcement actions under California's SB 942.
