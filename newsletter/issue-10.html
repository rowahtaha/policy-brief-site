Issue No. 10 · Week of November 3–13, 2026

This Week, Power Wrote Its Own Rules. Money Got New Ones Anyway.

By Rowa Taha

A CEO's personal life just erased $1.76 trillion in market value, a Michigan township's "no" got overturned by lawsuit before the ink dried, and three tech executives sat down with G7 leaders like it was the most normal thing in the world. Meanwhile, three different countries finished writing new rules for fintech products that have been running without much of a rulebook at all. Same week, two very different stories about who actually gets to decide.

01
Accountability: United States

Nvidia's CEO Quit After an Affair With a Suspected Chinese Spy. Here's Why Your 401(k) Should Care.

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO on November 12, days after US intelligence officials flagged his relationship with Alice Huang, a Singapore-based tech executive they suspect was tasked by Beijing with extracting intelligence on advanced AI chip technology.[1][2] Nvidia's board accepted the resignation the same day; CFO Colette Kress stepped in as interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent replacement.[3]

The market reaction was immediate and brutal: Nvidia has lost $1.76 trillion in value since the news broke, a sum larger than the entire market capitalization of most companies on Earth.[1] Congress has summoned Huang to testify, and lawmakers on both sides are treating this less as a personnel matter than as a national-security breach that happened to occur inside the most important company in the AI supply chain.[2]

This isn't really a story about one executive's judgment. Nvidia makes the chips that every major AI lab, and a large share of the world's pension funds and index funds, now depend on. When a security lapse at the top of that company can erase $1.76 trillion overnight, "it's just a personnel issue" stops being a credible way to describe what happened. If you hold index funds, a 401(k), or a pension, you already owned a piece of this before you read this sentence.

02
AI Governance: Global, G7

Tech CEOs Got a Seat at the G7 Table. Elected Governments Didn't Get Much Back.

At the G7 summit in Évian this past June, Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Demis Hassabis joined a formal working lunch alongside heads of state and government, one of the only times in G7 history that private company executives have sat inside the summit itself rather than meeting on its sidelines.[1]

Amodei and Hassabis used the access to push a specific ask: a US-led AI coalition built on structured access to frontier models for allied governments, paired with chip trade policy that deliberately excludes China.[2] Altman floated a softer alternative, an international testing-and-standards forum rather than a formal coalition. Neither pitch produced a binding commitment, and no elected body set the lunch's agenda or published minutes of what was actually discussed.[3]

Governments have spent years arguing over who gets to write the rules for AI. This is what it looks like when the people who built the technology simply show up and ask for a seat at the table where those rules get decided, and get it. Whether that's good-faith access or a quiet admission that the G7 can't govern this without the companies in the room is the real question the summit left unanswered.

03
Legislation: European Union

Brussels Just Gave Big Tech 16 More Months Before the EU AI Act's Real Rules Kick In

The European Parliament voted to delay the AI Act's high-risk system obligations, the rules governing hiring algorithms, credit scoring, and biometric sorting, from August 2026 to December 2027.[1] A separate, smaller carve-out pushes compliance for AI embedded in physical products to August 2028, and a new ban on "nudifier" apps takes effect this December as part of the same package.[2]

Industry pushback over compliance costs drove the delay, and it's officially being framed as "simplification" rather than retreat.[3] Three separate clocks got reset in the same bill at once: the high-risk deadline, the transparency and watermarking rules, and the deadline for countries to even build their AI testing infrastructure. I wrote a longer take on this one on Rowa's Thoughts, including checking whether it's actually the first time this deadline's been pushed. It is, for whatever that's worth.

It's 16 more months where hiring tools, credit scoring systems, and biometric sorting software built for high-stakes decisions about real people keep operating under the old, looser rules, sold to the public as a simplification rather than what it actually is: more time for the industry least eager to be regulated.

04
Data Rights: Nigeria, Kenya

Nigeria and Kenya Are Regulating AI Without Writing a Single AI Law

Neither Nigeria nor Kenya has passed standalone AI legislation, so both countries are leaning on their existing data-protection laws instead.[1] Kenya is tightening the rules governing data controllers and processors in ways that constrain how AI systems can be built and operated, without the text ever using the word "AI."

Nigeria is taking a similar path, folding AI and social-media oversight into its existing data-protection framework while a separate proposed National AI Commission bill works its way through the legislative process.[2] Neither approach was designed from the ground up to regulate AI specifically; both are retrofits of privacy law built for a different purpose.

When a continent's two biggest tech economies can't get dedicated AI bills through their own legislatures, data-rights law becomes the only lever anyone's actually pulling. Worth flagging plainly: this is a trend observed across reporting rather than one dated event, and only two independent sources cover it directly so far, so it's one to keep watching rather than treat as fully settled.

05
Legislation: Italy

Italy's AI Law Just Got Its Teeth: Who Enforces What Took Effect This Month

Italy passed the first comprehensive national AI law among EU member states back in 2025, with prison terms of one to five years for AI misuse like deepfakes and parental-consent requirements for anyone using AI services under 14.[1] For months, the law existed without anyone formally assigned to enforce it.

That changed October 23, when the implementing decree assigning enforcement power took effect, splitting authority between Italy's national cybersecurity agency and its digital-agency regulator.[2] The decree is the unglamorous administrative step that turns a law from a press release into something with a regulator actually standing behind it.[3]

It's a useful reminder that passing a law and being able to enforce it are two entirely different milestones, and the gap between them, over a year in Italy's case, is where a law actually becomes real or just becomes a headline that nothing follows up on.

06
Data Centers: United States, Michigan

A Michigan Town Voted No on an OpenAI Data Center. The Developer Sued Within Two Days, and Won.

Saline Township's board rejected a rezoning request for a Stargate-linked OpenAI-Oracle data center by a 4-1 vote in September 2025.[1] Related Digital, the developer, sued for exclusionary zoning within 48 hours of the vote.

The township settled within weeks, agreeing to community benefits including $4 million for farmland preservation and $8 million for fire services.[2] Construction began with Michigan Governor Gretchen Whitmer, Sam Altman, and Oracle's CEO all present at the June groundbreaking, less than a year after residents' own elected board had said no.[3]

At least 19 Michigan municipalities have since passed their own moratoriums on data-center development, having watched a local vote get overturned by a lawsuit before the ink on the rejection was even dry. That's the real lesson of Saline Township for any other town board about to hold a similar vote: a "no" is a starting position in the negotiation now, not necessarily the ending one.

07
Legislation: United Kingdom, European Union

Two Governments Are Trying to Regulate AI Chatbots Before a Child Gets Hurt by One

The UK confirmed in July that AI chatbot providers will face mandatory "breaks" for users under 18 and an 18-plus age floor for romantic or sexual roleplay bots, though the detailed rules on what a "break" actually requires haven't been written yet.[1]

The EU's proposed KIDS Act, unveiled September 17, goes further: companion chatbots and conversational AI would have to be off by default for minors and legally barred from designing in emotional dependency, with fines reportedly reaching 6% of a company's global turnover.[2] Neither government has set a compliance deadline yet.[3]

Both are reacting to the same underlying gap: a chatbot isn't a social-media feed, and most existing child-safety law was written for a world of posts and likes, not a conversational AI designed to keep a lonely teenager talking to it for as long as possible.

08
Fintech: European Union

Europe's Buy-Now-Pay-Later Rules Apply Continent-Wide Starting This Week

The EU's second Consumer Credit Directive, which pulls Buy Now, Pay Later loans under real credit regulation for the first time, starts applying November 20, regardless of whether individual member states have actually finished writing it into their own national law.[1]

Twenty-three of the EU's 27 member states missed last year's transposition deadline and received formal warning letters from the European Commission back in January.[2] Starting this week, Klarna and other BNPL lenders face the same affordability-check and disclosure duties as ordinary credit providers, whether or not their home government finished the paperwork.

This closes a loophole that let short-term installment loans skip consumer-protection law entirely for years, on the theory that four payments spread over six weeks wasn't really "credit." The EU just decided, continent-wide, that it is.[3]

09
Fintech: United States

US Regulators Just Finalized the Rulebook for Stablecoin Issuers

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued its final rule implementing the GENIUS Act on October 21, becoming the first US banking regulator to finalize stablecoin rules and setting reserve, custody, and disclosure requirements for issuers under its jurisdiction.[1]

The final version dropped a proposed automatic seven-day grace period that had been in the draft rule, while keeping the statutory ban on paying yield just for holding a stablecoin.[2] Full compliance is required by January 18, 2027, giving issuers a little over a year to actually build the infrastructure the rule demands.[3]

This is the regulatory scaffolding the US stablecoin market has been operating without for years, arriving all at once rather than gradually, which means the next year is really a test of whether issuers built for a lightly-regulated market can retrofit themselves into a heavily-regulated one in time.

10
Fintech: Kenya

Kenya Wants Stablecoin Issuers to Keep Nearly a Third of Their Reserves at Home

Kenya's draft Virtual Asset Service Providers Regulations would require stablecoin issuers to keep at least 30% of customer funds in segregated accounts at Kenyan banks, with the remainder held in cash or short-term government securities.[1]

Exchanges are pushing back, warning the rule could trap liquidity and raise costs, an argument that lands differently given Kenya's entire stablecoin market is currently estimated at roughly $3.85 million.[2] The rule is still a draft working through Kenya's Parliament, not yet final law.[3]

It's a small-economy answer to a problem that the GENIUS Act in the US and MiCA in the EU, both written for much larger financial systems, don't really have to think about: what happens to your own banking system when residents start moving their savings into a currency peg controlled entirely offshore.
