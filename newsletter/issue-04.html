Issue No. 4 · Week of September 14–20, 2026

A Law on the Books Isn't a System That Works

By Rowa Taha

Every story this week turns on the same gap: the distance between a rule existing and a rule actually working — and it shows up in strikingly different forms, from a fine that arrived four years too late to a policy that's two years old and still mostly on paper.

⚖️Lawsuit — United States
01

Meta's $18B Child-Safety Settlement Has a Catch

Meta agreed August 26 to a settlement worth up to $18 billion (52 state attorneys general, $12.7B guaranteed plus $5.3B contingent) over claims it designed products to be addictive to minors and collected under-13 data without consent. Meta's Chief Legal Officer C.J. Mahoney: "Because teens move fluidly across dozens of apps, we need an industry-wide solution" — but that solution only pays out in full if Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube adopt matching restrictions (one-hour daily limits, night mode, age assurance) and chip in equally. TikTok has already rejected Meta's ad campaign pressuring it to join.

⚖️Lawsuit — United Kingdom
02

Largest-Ever Collective Settlement Against Google

The Competition Appeal Tribunal approved a record £260 million settlement on September 16 between Google and thousands of UK app developers who alleged Google abused its Play Store dominance, ending a ten-week trial twelve days before it was set to open. £160 million goes directly to developers — "the largest collective action payout the Tribunal has ever approved," per lead claimant Professor Barry Rodger. Google admits no liability, and the tribunal made no finding on whether its commission was actually excessive.

⚡Data Centers — Global
03

Tech Giants Say They've Solved AI's Water Problem. An Expert Says Not So Fast.

Nvidia, Microsoft, AWS, and Meta all told AFP their newest data centers use closed-loop cooling with "no net water loss," even as Rystad Energy projects global data-center water use will nearly triple from 222 billion litres in 2025 to as much as 644 billion by 2030 — AWS alone ranges from 0.02 litres/kWh in Stockholm to 2.85 in Jakarta, a 100-fold spread. Independent researcher Andy Masley and Professor Shaolei Ren both note the catch: cutting water use usually means burning more electricity instead, and since water is far cheaper than power, companies have little cost incentive to actually reduce total consumption — only PR pressure.

🔒Data Rights — Chile
04

A Landmark Privacy Law Gets Delayed Before It Even Starts

Chile's government asked its Senate on September 1 to push back the effective date of its flagship data protection law (No. 21,719) by a full year, from December 2026 to December 2027 — not because of industry pushback, but because the Senate itself rejected the government's first slate of nominees for the new Data Protection Agency's council back in May, and the legal deadline to appoint them expired in June. The rights, obligations, and fines in the law don't change; only when — or whether — anyone will actually be checking compliance does.

🤖AI Governance — Africa (Continental)
05

A Continental AI Strategy, Two Years In, Still Mostly on Paper

The African Union endorsed a Continental AI Strategy in July 2024 urging all 55 member states to build national AI governance frameworks. Two years later, only 13 national AI strategies exist continent-wide (Rwanda led in 2019, most recently Kenya in May 2025), and roughly half of Africa hasn't even started drafting one. Researchers who interviewed African government officials found the gap isn't political resistance — it's that writing real technical AI policy requires specialist drafting capacity, institutional design, and dedicated budget lines most governments don't have yet. A separate analysis adds another gap: the strategy itself "did not adequately consider the perspectives of the most vulnerable users," including people with disabilities, women, and rural populations.

🏛️Legislation — Southeast Asia
06

The Under-16 Social Media Wave Spreads, Unevenly

Indonesia became the first Southeast Asian country to restrict under-16 social media access — Government Regulation No. 17, effective March 28, 2026, bars under-16s from "high-risk" platforms (YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, Roblox, Bigo Live) with fines and account termination for non-compliant platforms, phased in over two years and covering roughly 70 million children. Malaysia followed June 1 with mandatory age verification via MyKad national ID plus live facial biometric checks through MyDigital ID, backed by fines up to RM10 million. The Philippines is still just debating it. Same regional rule, three different speeds.

⚖️Lawsuit — United States
07

Authors Bet They Can Beat Anthropic's Own Settlement

Six authors led by two-time Pulitzer winner John Carreyrou opted out of Anthropic's already-approved $1.5B copyright settlement (which paid roughly $3,000/work) to sue Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Perplexity individually in the Northern District of California — seeking $150,000 per title, per company, and marking the first copyright suit against both xAI and Perplexity over training practices. Their complaint: the settlement "seems to serve [the AI companies], not creators." They're betting that going outside the negotiated system gets them more than staying inside it.

📰Accountability — Netherlands / EU
08

Uber Fined €825 Million — for Conduct That Ended Four Years Ago

The Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Uber €824.99 million on August 21 — the second-largest GDPR fine ever, after Meta's €1.2B — for automatically deactivating drivers over fraud suspicions or low ratings with no human review. Deputy chair Monique Verdier: "A computer should not make decisions on its own that have major consequences for you. These decisions should have been looked at first by a human being." The case traces back to 171 French drivers who filed a complaint in 2020 over conduct that ran from 2018 to 2022. Uber stopped the practice years ago; the fine — 1.85% of its global turnover — only arrived this year.

📰Accountability — Uganda
09

National ID Data Leaked to a Trafficking Ring

A former registration assistant at Uganda's National Identification and Registration Authority, Douglas Kaaya, was charged and remanded after allegedly using his access to leak national ID data on young Ugandan women to a trafficking syndicate that lured them to the UAE with fake job offers — exposed by a BBC documentary. He's charged under Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act, facing up to 10 years. The law existed the whole time; it didn't stop the leak, only makes it prosecutable after the fact.

🤖AI Governance — Africa (Continental)
10

"We Don't Lack Policy, We Lack a System That Works Together"

At the Hyperscalers Convergence Africa 2026 conference in Lagos (September 4), officials including Dr Talkmore Chidede of the African Continental Free Trade Area Secretariat and representatives from Nigeria's NITDA and Egypt's Ministry of Communications argued that Africa's real problem isn't a shortage of digital policy — it has the Malabo Convention, a Digital Transformation Strategy, an AfCFTA Digital Trade Protocol since February 2024. "Africa does not lack digital policy," said Chidera Ike-Okonkwo of Nina Jojer's Africa policy team. "What we lack instead is a system in which these policies work together." The proposed fix: interoperability, not uniformity — let countries keep their own laws, but agree on minimum standards so the systems can actually talk to each other.

What This Means

Every one of this week's ten stories turns on the same gap: the distance between a rule existing and a rule actually working. And the striking thing is how many different ways that gap shows up.

Sometimes the gap is time. Uber's algorithm was firing drivers without human review from 2018 to 2022. The fine for it landed in August 2026 — four years after the conduct stopped, and six years after the first driver complained. The GDPR was already in force the entire time this was happening. The law wasn't the problem; the years it took an investigation to become a decision were. Uganda's story is a sharper version of the same lag: a national data-protection law existed the whole time a NIRA employee allegedly sold young women's identity data to traffickers. The law didn't prevent that harm — it only gives prosecutors something to charge him with now that the harm is already done.

Sometimes the gap is capacity, not time. Chile isn't delaying its privacy law because industry is fighting it — it's delaying because the government hasn't finished building the regulator that's supposed to enforce it. The African Union's continental AI strategy is two years old, and researchers who actually asked African officials why implementation is thin got a consistent answer: not indifference, but a real shortage of the drafting capacity, institutional design, and budget lines that turning a strategy into an enforceable system requires. And the Hyperscalers Africa panel put the sharpest possible frame on it: the continent doesn't lack policy, it lacks a system where the policies it already has actually talk to each other. Three stories, one throughline — you can declare a rule in an afternoon; building the machinery that makes it real takes years, specialists, and money that isn't always there.

And sometimes the gap is deliberate, dressed up as choice. Nvidia, Microsoft, AWS, and Meta all want you to believe closed-loop cooling has solved AI's water problem. The honest answer from the people who study this is that water and electricity trade off against each other, and companies have no binding requirement to reduce either — just PR incentive to look like they have. That's not a capacity gap. That's a system deliberately left voluntary, because voluntary is what a company chooses when it doesn't want to be held to anything.

Which is what makes the Meta and Google settlements, and the authors' lawsuits, worth reading as a group. In every one of those, somebody explicitly gave up on waiting for "the system" — a comprehensive law, a functioning regulator, a class settlement everyone stays inside of — and used litigation to force a concrete outcome instead: real usage limits for teenagers, a real £160 million payout, a real bet that six authors can extract more suing individually than 500,000 rights-holders got collectively. Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines are all writing the same under-16 social media rule at three different speeds, which is its own quiet argument that even when everyone agrees on the rule, building the system to actually run it is the part that takes real time.

Worth watching next: whether TikTok and YouTube actually join Meta's settlement or let $5 billion of it evaporate; whether Chile's one-year delay becomes a second one once 2027 arrives; and whether the Hyperscalers Africa "interoperability, not uniformity" pitch gets any real institutional backing, or joins the pile of frameworks it's specifically describing.
