The Council of Europe AI Convention: Anchoring AI in Centuries of Human Rights Struggle

Rowa Taha · November 16, 2025 · 9 min read

The development of CETS No. 225 was a process that, while formally initiated in 2019, drew its philosophical strength from a lineage of laws designed to protect human dignity. The Council of Europe (CoE), founded in 1949 in the aftermath of two devastating World Wars, exists explicitly to uphold the bedrock principles of human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.

This Convention is thus built upon foundational European legal instruments, most notably the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) and Convention 108 (the world's first legally binding international data protection treaty from 1981).

The process itself was remarkably thorough and intentional:

  • A Multi-Year Effort: Work began in 2019 with the ad hoc Committee on Artificial Intelligence (CAHAI), leading to the drafting phase by the Committee on Artificial Intelligence (CAI) and its adoption in May 2024.
  • Global Stakeholder Engagement: The text was drafted and negotiated not only by the 46 CoE member states but also with the participation of non-member observer states, including the United States, Canada, Japan, Mexico, and others. Crucially, 68 representatives from civil society, academia, and industry were involved, ensuring a broad and balanced perspective.

By embedding AI regulation within this historical context a context shaped by revolutions, conflicts, colonization and the ongoing struggle for fundamental freedoms, the Convention treats AI not as a mere technical problem, but as a challenge to maintain human rights protections.

I. From Voluntary Ethics to Mandatory International Law

Perhaps the most significant differentiator of the Council of Europe Convention is its status as a legally binding treaty. In the contemporary AI governance landscape, which is littered with "soft law," guidelines, and non-binding ethical declarations, CETS No. 225 makes a profound and necessary legal statement.

Elevating the Standard of Governance

Most international AI agreements such as the OECD AI Principles or declarations arising from the G7's Hiroshima AI Process are valuable, yet ultimately voluntary. They rely on the goodwill and self-regulation of states and corporations. CETS No. 225 shatters this model:

  • Mandatory Legislative Action: Once a state ratifies the Convention, it is legally obliged to "adopt or maintain appropriate legislative, administrative or other measures" to enforce the Convention’s principles within its national legal system.
  • Enforceable Rights: This transforms concepts like "fairness" and "transparency" from mere ethical aspirations into enforceable legal rights. Citizens gain a clear legal basis to challenge algorithmic decisions that violate their fundamental rights.
  • Global Reach and Influence: The open nature of the treaty allows it to set a minimum global floor for AI ethics. By securing the participation of non-European nations with massive AI sectors (like the US, Canada, and Japan), the CoE effectively exports its high human rights standards beyond its geographic borders.

This shift from voluntary commitment to binding law is a crucial step in maturing AI governance, ensuring that accountability is not optional but a fundamental requirement.

II. Key Aspects of the Framework

The Convention’s requirements are systematic, covering the entire lifecycle of an AI system, from its initial planning and design to its eventual deployment and decommissioning. Its core mandate is simple: AI systems must be compatible with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.

The countries that have signed this landmark agreement are Andorra, Canada, Georgia, Iceland, Israel, Japan, Liechtenstein, the Republic of Moldova, Montenegro, Norway, San Marino, Switzerland, Ukraine, the United Kingdom, the United States of America, and Uruguay, with the European Union also signing on behalf of its member states. This list shows a significant commitment from nations both inside and outside the Council of Europe to set a global standard for AI governance that prioritizes human rights and democratic values.

A. Core Principles and Values

Signatory parties commit to ensuring that their use of AI adheres to the following core principles:

  1. Human Dignity and Individual Autonomy: Protecting the human identity and ability of individuals to make free choices, uncoerced or unduly influenced by algorithmic systems.
  2. Equality and Non-Discrimination: Requiring specific measures to prevent AI systems from perpetuating or exacerbating systemic bias and discrimination, with specific mention of gender equality.
  3. Respect for Privacy and Personal Data Protection: Strengthening existing data protection frameworks to ensure control over personal information used to train and operate AI.
  4. Transparency and Oversight: Mandating clear documentation of AI systems, their intended purpose, and their risks.
  5. Accountability and Responsibility: Establishing clear lines of responsibility for any harms caused by AI systems, ensuring that accountability is not outsourced to the technology itself.
  6. Reliability and Safety: Ensuring systems function reliably, robustly, and safely in their intended operational context.
  7. Safe Innovation: Obliging states to enable controlled environments (like regulatory sandboxes) for testing new systems responsibly.

B. Risk Management and Oversight

The treaty adopts a systemic approach to risk, requiring proactive mitigation measures:

  • Impact and Risk Assessments: Signatories must implement mechanisms requiring deployers of AI to conduct thorough risk and impact assessments to anticipate and mitigate potential negative effects on human rights and democracy before deployment.
  • The Power to Halt: States must maintain the legal authority to impose bans or moratoria on certain AI applications if the identified risks are deemed incompatible with fundamental rights or the integrity of democratic processes.
  • Transparency Requirements: Developers and deployers must document relevant information about the AI system, including its design, functioning, and training data. A key provision is the obligation to ensure people are notified when they are interacting with an AI system rather than a human being.

C. Remedies and Accountability

The Convention is unequivocal that technological innovation cannot negate the right to justice:

  • Accessible and Effective Remedies: The most significant safeguard is the mandate that when human rights are violated, states must provide accessible and effective remedies.
  • Right to Challenge: This includes ensuring affected persons have sufficient information and procedural means to challenge decisions made by or substantially based on an AI system, reversing the traditional power imbalance between the citizen and the algorithm.

III. Impact on Big Tech, Society, and the Economy

The Framework Convention's influence extends far beyond legal theory. Its impact on the business of big tech, its benefits for society, and its overall effect on the European economy present a complex balance.

The Dual Scope and Big Tech

The most debated aspect of the Convention is its tiered application:

  • Public Sector Mandatory: The treaty applies fully and mandatorily to the use of AI systems by public authorities. This guarantees that government services, from justice to healthcare, adhere to the highest rights standards.
  • Private Sector Flexibility: For private entities (developers, large tech companies, start-ups), signatories are granted flexibility. They must "address risks and impacts" from private actors, either by applying the Convention’s principles directly or by taking "other appropriate measures".

This flexibility was necessary to secure the signatures of countries like the US, which typically resist mandatory private sector regulation. However, for Big Tech operating in Europe, the Convention acts as a powerful reinforcing mechanism alongside the EU AI Act. This combination strengthens the "Brussels Effect," forcing global companies to conform to high European human rights and accountability standards to retain access to the massive European market, regardless of where the AI is developed.

Assessing the Good for Society, People, and the Economy

The assessment of the Convention’s overall impact requires a balanced view. For society and the people, the Convention is a major net positive. It formalizes safeguards against algorithmic injustice, discrimination, and the erosion of democratic principles that all took a long time to from. It creates a clear legal baseline for human rights protection in the digital sphere, provides citizens with a clear right to redress, and fosters public trust through mandatory transparency. It also directly protects democratic institutions and judicial processes from algorithmic abuse.

From an economic perspective, the impact is more nuanced. The mandatory requirements for risk assessments, documentation, and technical transparency will undoubtedly raise compliance costs for developers, which could slow innovation initially, especially for smaller companies. However, the Convention explicitly includes the principle of "Safe Innovation," which obliges states to enable controlled environments (like regulatory sandboxes) for testing new systems responsibly. This balanced approach aims to mitigate economic friction. Ultimately, by creating a predictable, stable, and rights-L-respecting legal environment, the Convention fosters the public trust necessary for the sustainable, long-term growth of the AI market.

IV. Real-World Tremors: Big Tech's Preparatory Compliance and Political Engagement

Since the Council of Europe Convention (CETS No. 225) was adopted only in May 2024 and requires ratification by nations to take full legal effect, we cannot yet point to enforcement actions or fines under its name. However, the treaty's mandatory nature and unique focus on fundamental human rights have already forced major changes in Big Tech's strategic and compliance planning.

  • Strategic Engagement in Drafting: Companies like Google, Microsoft, and Meta were heavily involved in the multi-year drafting process as part of observer delegations (including the US and Canada). This allowed the CoE to directly communicate the core bans, such as on algorithmic social scoring and manipulative AI, and forced these major players to grapple with the need to incorporate these human rights "red lines" into their Responsible AI principles globally, well before the Convention’s full legal entry into force.
  • Principle-Driven Product Alignment (Microsoft & OpenAI): The Convention demands robust Accountability and Reliability and Transparency and Oversight. In anticipation of these binding rules, companies like Microsoft and their partners, such as OpenAI, are publicly emphasizing the creation of extensive documentation and risk assessments for their General Purpose AI (GPAI) models. Their preparatory compliance focuses on the Convention's principles of human oversight and effective remedies, ensuring their enterprise platforms can provide the necessary audit trails and documentation for future regulatory review in member states.
  • Mandatory Transparency on Synthetic Media (Meta & Google): The Convention's emphasis on Transparency and Oversight directly addresses the creation of AI-generated content (like deepfakes). In direct alignment with the Convention's goal of protecting the democratic process, Big Tech platforms like Meta (Facebook, Instagram) and Google (YouTube, Gemini) are pre-emptively implementing mandatory watermarking and labeling protocols for synthetic media. This anticipates the Convention's requirement that people must be notified when they are interacting with an AI system or AI-generated content.
  • Global Policy Shift: The unprecedented participation of non-European nations (US, Japan, Canada) in the Convention's final text ensures that the CoE's principles are now part of the global dialogue. For Big Tech, this means that compliance is no longer a localized European problem; the foundational human rights requirements for AI must now be integrated into their global design and deployment strategies to be prepared for the inevitable ratification across diverse international markets.

💡 My Opinion

It is crucial to understand this Convention not as a universal blueprint for mandatory adoption, but as an indispensable international baseline. While the Convention's open nature and high standards are valuable, true, effective governance requires that countries worldwide resist the pressure to blindly adopt foreign laws. Every nation, whether highly developed or still emerging, must instead take the time and make the effort to create custom-made, sovereignty-respecting domestic legislation for AI, and everything else. This is vital because effective law must be tailored to the country's specific economic goals, unique social norms, and long, complex history. The Coe Convention provides a robust reference point, but national success in AI governance will ultimately rely on this commitment to localized, context-aware legal design.
