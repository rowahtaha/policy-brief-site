Is Technology Alone the Key to Prosperity?

Rowa Taha · November 17, 2025 · 2 min read

The 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics, awarded to Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, reminds us of a crucial truth: technology advancement has never made societies more prosperous without the right social and political institutions.

I can’t stop thinking about this in today’s world. We have actual cures for cancer, but they’re often and purposefully too expensive for most people. Does a world with these technologies fundamentally change the poverty divide if access is not included in the equation?

The entire logic of U.S. IP law is a clear economic bargain: grant limited-time exclusive rights (patents, copyrights) as an incentive for invention, and in return, the discovery eventually enters the public domain, making it cheaper and widely accessible.

But this bargain is often broken, not just by market forces, but by political ones:

-The Economic Reality: The primary incentive behind new discovery is always economic gain, leading companies to engage in “evergreening” — filing new patents on minor tweaks to prolong monopoly pricing far beyond the original term.

-The Digital Barrier: Modern technologies require high access infrastructure (fast internet, up-to-date devices) that creates an inherent tax on access, preventing true “fair use.”

-The Geopolitical Chokehold: Beyond the economic factors, global superpowers increasingly impose export controls and strategic restrictions on cutting-edge or “dual-use” technologies (like advanced chips, AI, or specific clean energy systems). For political or national security reasons, countries actively prevent key inventions from being shared or sold to certain nations. This is especially true for the Global South, which is often caught in the crossfire of this technological rivalry, denied access to tools crucial for health, climate adaptation, and development.

The fact is, even when an IP right legally expires, the innovation rarely translates directly into broad public prosperity.

If we want technology to truly “increase the prosperity of the world” and narrow the poverty divide, we have to stop focusing only on the invention and start prioritizing social innovation.

This means rejecting blanket, one-size-fits-all laws and instead designing contextual institutions tailored to each nation’s unique fabric.

Social innovation is making laws that adopt technologies based on where the country is at according to diverse measures, including its economic capacity, social values, history, and anthropology.

Think of it like an ecosystem: when a species from an entirely different place is suddenly introduced, it’s bound to be destructive. Technology, particularly something as transformative as AI, is a foreign species. Every country must carefully measure: Does this new thing fit my societal ecosystem, or will it be destructive?
