Mercor : How a $10 Billion Startup is Erasing Professionals

Rowa Taha · January 27, 2026 · 5 min read

I recently graduated with my LLM in Tech Policy, a degree that represents a year of academic waterboarding, preceded by five years of legal practice in corporate law firms in Sudan. I returned to the job market expecting a professional dialogue, or at the very least, a conversation with a human being who understood the "law" is more than just a series of if-then statements. Instead, I was ghosted by the entire species and replaced by a link to a Mercor "AI Interview."

I was appalled. The insult was visceral. it felt like after years of law school, working and re-adjusting to the American system doing all those mock interviews meant nothing. In the eyes of this company, all that mattered was how I scored against this algorithm in a 22 minute AI interview. Ofc i didn't apply. I don't want to work for a company that won't dignify me with a human interview.

The Bias in the Machine

As a specialist in AI governance, I know exactly why my gut reacted with such hostility. These systems aren't objective; they are automated repositories of human prejudice. When Mercor’s "AI agent" scans a candidate, it isn't looking for talent; it is looking for a mirror of its biased training data. Furthermore, the data itself is inherently limited, narrowly sourced and stripped of the messy, real-world context that defines professional excellence, which ensures the algorithm is biased by design.

As someone who comes from Africa, this digital gatekeeping feels particularly predatory. Research suggests that only about 2% of LLM training data is representative of African contexts. This statistical invisibility puts me at an immediate disadvantage before the "interview" even starts. I am being measured against a Western-centric "ideal" by a machine that has barely been exposed to the linguistic, cultural, and professional nuances of my home.

The ways these algorithms fail us are specific, systemic, and frankly, cruel:

  • The Linguistic Glass Ceiling: These models are trained on a narrow "standard" of professional speech. If you have a cultural accent or a non-standard cadence, the AI docks you for "clarity," effectively automating a high-tech version of xenophobia.
  • The Neurotypical Trap: By tracking eye contact and micro-expressions, the system criminalizes non-standard biology. For neurodivergent professionals, a lack of "standard" eye contact is hallucinated by the AI as a lack of confidence or honesty.
  • Affective Fallacy: These tools attempt to map facial movements to emotional states, an idea that is scientifically garbage. It fails to account for the simple reality of human nerves or the focused intensity of a practitioner.
  • Socio-Economic Signifiers: The AI often scans your background. It rewards those with the luxury of a pristine, high-end home office and penalizes those without, turning the interview into a wealth-screen before you've even opened your mouth.
  • Contextual Blindness: AI lacks the "common sense" to understand intent or sarcasm. It processes words as tokens rather than meaning, missing the strategic depth that a seasoned lawyer or policy expert brings to the table.
  • Algorithmic Hallucinations: Large Language Models are notorious for confidently stating falsehoods. When applied to recruitment, this means the system can "hallucinate" gaps in a resume or invent personality flaws that simply do not exist.
  • The Illusion of Accountability: Until recently, a machine's decision, based on the tilt of your head or the speed of your typing, remains a nearly immutable professional verdict with no clear path for appeal.
  • Data Persistence and Security: Once your biometrics are captured, they are rarely truly deleted. This creates a permanent digital shadow, a "permanent record" that can follow you across platforms and future job applications without your consent.

The $10 Billion Data Harvest

Mercor is currently the darling of the VC world, valued at $10 billion. But for those of us on the other side of the screen, the platform feels like a high-stakes Facade.

Rumors within the tech community suggest the "interview" is actually a data harvest. Under the guise of "pre-vetted talent," Mercor captures your face, voice, and unique intellectual responses, your very biometrics, to train the next generation of LLMs.

In this cycle, you aren't the candidate; you are the training data. You are performing for a machine so that your human essence can be extracted to teach AI how to sound more like a professional. It is the ultimate flex: using your search for a livelihood to fuel the technology that aims to render your expertise obsolete.

The Global Right to be Repelled

We’ve entered a sterile ghost dance where companies use AI to filter talent and applicants use AI to bypass those filters. It’s a loop of machines talking to machines, leaving the human professional shouting into a void.

However, the tide is turning. Under the EU AI Act, hiring is classified as "high-risk," granting individuals a right to explainability. This isn't just a European phenomenon; the global regulatory machine is waking up to the indignity of the automated interview:

  • United States: Illinois led with the Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act, requiring transparency, consent, and the destruction of video data within 30 days of a candidate’s request. New York City’s Local Law 144 mandates annual bias audits for hiring tools, and California is moving to treat AI bias as a fundamental civil rights violation.
  • Brazil: The landmark Bill No. 2338/2023 (The Brazilian AI Act) establishes clear rights for those affected by AI, including the right to meaningful information about automated decisions and a human-in-the-loop review process for high-risk systems like recruitment.
  • South Korea: With the AI Framework Act of 2025, Korea has become a leader in the APAC region, mandating that "high-impact" systems provide clear explanations for their results and decision criteria.

The right to explainability should not be a regional luxury; it should be a standard human right. Candidates subjected to AI interviews must have an absolute right to have their data deleted and, crucially, the power to opt out of these automated gauntlets entirely without prejudice.

Beyond the legalities, there is a fundamental truth we must confront: AI algorithms are simply not good enough to be used this way yet. They lack the nuance to measure human potential. But even if the technology were perfect, it would still be an affront to human dignity to be forced through a machine-led gauntlet just to prove your worth. If a firm won't look you in the eye or even let you through the digital door, walk away. Our careers are too valuable to be left to the mercy of a $10 billion answering machine that hallucinates our worth while harvesting us for its next update.
